### [1] [Inventories of Rich Carbon-Chain Chemistry in Prestellar and Starless Cores in the Perseus Molecular Cloud](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05264)
*Anissa Pokorny-Yadav,Samantha Scibelli,Judit Ferrer Asensio,Yancy Shirley,Andrés Megías,Izaskun Jiménez-Serra*

Main category: astro-ph.GA

TL;DR: This study analyzes the carbon-chain molecular inventories of 15 prestellar and starless cores in the Perseus Molecular Cloud using Yebes 40m single-dish observations, detecting species like CS, CCS, CCCS, HC3N, DC3N, HC5N, and HC7N along with multiple isotopologues. The authors report detection statistics, compare column density ratios with other regions, examine deuterium fractionation, and investigate correlations between cyanopolyynes and sulfur-bearing carbon-chains, revealing how local environmental conditions shape each core's chemical inventory and evolutionary phase.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Carbon-chain molecules are important reservoirs of reactive organic matter that eventually get incorporated into protoplanetary disks, planets, and cometary material. While surveys of starless cores have focused on complex organic molecules (COMs), observations of carbon-chains in starless cores remain limited, motivating this systematic study of carbon-chain chemistry in prestellar and starless cores.

Method: Yebes 40m single-dish observations of 15 prestellar and starless cores in the Perseus Molecular Cloud. The study includes detection statistics of carbon-chain species (CS, CCS, CCCS, HC3N, DC3N, HC5N, HC7N) and their isotopologues (including 13CS, C34S, H13CCCN, DCCCCCN, etc.), comparison of column density ratios with Taurus, Serpens, and protostar sources, DC3N/HC3N deuterium fractionation analysis, and correlation studies between cyanopolyyne (HCnN) and sulfur-bearing (CnS) chains.

Result: Carbon-chain species (CS, CCS, CCCS, HC3N, DC3N, HC5N) were detected in at least 10 of 15 cores, with HC7N detected in 4 of 15 cores. Numerous isotopologues including carbon, sulfur, and nitrogen substituted variants as well as deuterated species were detected. The diverse suite of detected species reveals the richness of carbon-chain chemistry in Perseus and demonstrates how local conditions (density, temperature, proximity to protostellar activity) shape each core's molecular inventory and relative evolutionary phase.

Conclusion: The findings provide insight into the carbon-chain reservoir of starless and prestellar cores in Perseus, which may ultimately be inherited by emerging protoplanetary disks and later integrated into planetary systems and biologically relevant material, highlighting the importance of carbon-chain chemistry in the earliest stages of star and planet formation.

Abstract: Carbon-chain molecules serve as an important reservoir of reactive organic matter that will eventually be incorporated into protoplanetary disks, planets, and cometary material. Prestellar and starless cores are composed of cold (~ 10 K) and dense (~ 10$^5$ cm$^{-3}$) clumps of gas and dust within molecular clouds, and are nurseries for low-mass stars and planetary systems. Surveys of starless cores have focused on the study of complex organic molecules, COMs, whereas observations of carbon-chains in starless cores are limited. We analyze the carbon-chain inventories of 15 prestellar and starless cores in the Perseus Molecular Cloud. Using Yebes 40m single-dish observations, we detect CS, CCS, CCCS, HC$_3$N, DC$_3$N, and HC$_5$N in at least 10/15 cores and HC$_7$N in 4/15 cores. Our study also finds related isotopologues, where $^{13}$CS, C$^{34}$S, C$^{13}$CS, CC$^{34}$S, H$^{13}$CCCN, HC$^{13}$CCN, HCC$^{13}$CN, HC$^{13}$CCCCN, HCC$^{13}$CCCN, HCCC$^{13}$CCN, HCCCCC$^{15}$N, and DCCCCCN are detected. We report detection statistics, compare column density ratios with Taurus, Serpens, and protostar sources, examine DC$_3$N/HC$_3$N deuterium fractionation, and investigate the relative abundances and correlations between cyanopolyyne (HC$_n$N) and sulfur-bearing (C$_n$S) carbon-chains. The diverse suite of species detected reveals the richness of carbon-chain chemistry in Perseus and illustrates how local environmental conditions, such as density, temperature, and proximity to protostellar activity, shape each core's molecular inventory and relative evolutionary phase. Our findings provide a glimpse into the carbon-chain reservoir of starless and prestellar cores in the Perseus, which may ultimately be inherited by emerging protoplanetary disks and later integrated into planetary systems and biologically relevant material.

### [2] [Where did all the Little Red Dots go? The abundance of LRD analogues among objects with broad lines at $z < 0.35$](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05267)
*Lasse Seyberlich,Sarah E. I. Bosman*

Main category: astro-ph.GA

TL;DR: The paper searches for local (z<0.35) analogues of high-redshift "Little Red Dots" (LRDs) using SDSS broad-line objects, finding that 19% show v-shaped SEDs, and identifies nine local LRD analogues (0.08% of broad-line objects), including one previously known analogue ("Egg") and one object (J0302-0101) with all LRD hallmarks, highlighting that LRDs are extremely rare at low redshift.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: LRDs discovered by JWST at high redshift are puzzling due to their broad Balmer lines, compact morphologies, and v-shaped SEDs. Their high redshift makes observations expensive and difficult, so finding local analogues would enable cheaper, more detailed studies of the physical processes behind LRDs.

Method: Starting from an unbiased parent catalogue of all SDSS objects with broad Hα or Hβ emission lines (galaxies and quasars) at 0<z<0.35, complemented by archival photometry and spectroscopy, the authors searched for objects with v-shaped SEDs and selected those well-described by a modified black body (MBB) to identify local LRD analogues.

Result: Among broad-line objects, 19% show v-shaped SEDs. Nine local LRD analogues were identified (0.08% of all broad-line objects). Among objects matching typical LRD luminosities, 5% display a v-shape and 0.37% display a MBB-like SED. One previously-known analogue ("Egg") was recovered, validating the methodology. One new object (J0302-0101) shows all LRD hallmarks despite being embedded in a galaxy core.

Conclusion: LRDs are extremely rare at z<0.35, with very few broad-line objects showing LRD-like SEDs even without requiring compactness matching. The identification of nine local analogues provides valuable targets for detailed follow-up observations to understand LRD physics.

Abstract: One of the most puzzling discoveries since the launch of the James Webb Space Telescope is a numerous population of sources at high redshift nicknamed ``Little Red Dots'' (LRDs). Characterized by broad Balmer emission lines, compact morphologies and v-shaped spectral energy distributions (SEDs) in the rest-frame optical, the nature of LRDs remains elusive. A major challenge is the high redshift nature of LRDs, which makes observations very expensive and difficult. Finding local LRD analogues is a crucial step in unravelling the physical processes giving rise to LRDs, as cheaper observations with many facilities would become available. In this paper, we conduct a search for local LRD analogues by starting from an unbiased parent catalogue of all SDSS objects with broad H$α$ or H$β$ emission lines (both galaxies and quasars) at $0 < z < 0.35$, complemented by archival photometry and spectroscopy. We find that among broad-line objects, $19\%$ show a v-shaped SED. We then select objects whose SED is well described by a modified black body (MBB), and identify nine local LRD analogues, corresponding to $0.08\%$ of all objects with broad lines. Among only objects which match the typical continuum and emission line luminosities of LRDs, $5\%$ display a v-shape and $0.37\%$ display a MBB-like SED. We recover one previously-identified local LRD analogue (the so-called ``Egg''), validating our methodology. Among the other eight analogues, one (J0302$-$0101) shows all the hallmarks of an LRD despite being visibly embedded in the core of a galaxy; another object is ambiguous, and the remaining six may have alternative explanations for their peculiar SEDs. Our study highlights that LRDs are extremely rare at $z < 0.35$; we find very few objects with broad lines which have LRD-like SEDs, even without attempting to match the compactness of LRDs.

### [3] [Expanding stellar associations as Galactic accelerometers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05297)
*Till Sawala,Núria Miret Roig*

Main category: astro-ph.GA

TL;DR: This paper introduces a novel method to constrain the Milky Way's gravitational potential by using expanding young stellar associations. By tracing the orbits of stars that share a common, compact origin backward in time under different trial potentials, the true potential is identified as the one that minimizes the size metrics (trace and determinant) of the association's position covariance. Current observational errors limit this approach, but future data (Gaia DR4 astrometry, radial velocities < 0.2 km/s) could constrain halo mass to 0.6 trillion solar masses and concentration to < 0.8 with a single association. The work also highlights degeneracies and the potential role of independent age information to break them.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Understanding the Milky Way's gravitational potential is essential for studying Galactic evolution and dark matter. The authors seek a new, complementary method to constrain this potential, leveraging the physical constraint that stars in young stellar associations share a common, compact origin.

Method: The method uses expanding young stellar associations as dynamical tracers. Synthetic associations are integrated backward in time under different trial potentials, and the potential that minimizes the size metrics (trace and determinant of the position covariance matrix) is identified as the correct one. The approach is validated with synthetic data and evaluated for sensitivity to observational errors and degeneracies.

Result: Current observational errors are too large for this method to distinguish between potentials. However, with Gaia DR4 astrometry and radial velocity errors below 0.2 km/s, the halo mass can be constrained to a precision of 0.6 trillion solar masses and concentration to < 0.8 using a single association, though significant degeneracies exist. The inferred traceback age is sensitive to the assumed potential, meaning independent age information could help break degeneracies, but traceback ages are not independent of the assumed potential.

Conclusion: Expanding stellar associations carry information about the Galactic potential in which they evolved. With next-generation astrometry and high-precision radial velocities, they will provide a complementary tool for constraining the Milky Way's gravitational potential, with potential synergy from independent age information to resolve degeneracies.

Abstract: The gravitational potential of the Milky Way is fundamental for understanding the evolution of our Galaxy and the nature of dark matter. We introduce a new method to constrain the Galactic potential using expanding young stellar associations. We exploit the physical constraint that these stars share a common, compact origin to reconstruct their most likely orbits and infer the gravitational potential in which they have evolved. We define the size of an association using the trace and determinant of its position covariance matrix. By integrating synthetic associations backward in different trial potentials, we show how the true potential can be identified as the one that minimises these metrics. We demonstrate that, while current observational errors are still too large, upcoming observations will allow us to distinguish between different potentials. Our results suggest that with Gaia DR4 astrometry and radial velocity errors below 0.2 km/s, the halo mass can be constrained with a precision of 0.6 trillion solar masses and the concentration with a precision of < 0.8 using a single association, albeit with significant degeneracies. In addition, we show that the inferred dynamical traceback age is sensitive to the gravitational potential, suggesting that independent age information can help break existing degeneracies, but also that traceback-age estimates are not independent of the assumed potential. Expanding stellar associations carry information about the gravitational potential in which they have evolved. With the arrival of next-generation astrometry and high-precision radial velocities, they will provide a complementary tool for constraining the Galactic potential.

### [4] [Clump Migration in Disk Galaxies: Revisiting Chandrasekhar's Dynamical Friction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06350)
*Pushpak Pandey,Kanak Saha*

Main category: astro-ph.GA

TL;DR: The paper tests the classical Chandrasekhar dynamical friction formalism for predicting stellar clump migration toward galactic centers using an isolated disk galaxy simulation. By tracking nine long-lived clumps, the authors find that while the analytical model captures the general dependence on clump mass and radius, it overestimates inspiral times by factors of 2-10 for six clumps, suggesting additional physical processes beyond classical Chandrasekhar theory govern clump migration.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Massive stellar clumps are believed to migrate inward via dynamical friction, contributing to bulge growth and disk galaxy structure. However, the validity of the classical Chandrasekhar formalism for extended, evolving clumps in realistic galactic disks is uncertain, motivating a direct test against simulation data.

Method: The authors run an isolated disk galaxy simulation, develop a position- and mass-based tracking algorithm to identify and follow long-lived stellar clumps over multiple orbital periods, and compare their measured orbital evolution with predictions from a dynamical friction model constructed from the simulated baryonic and dark matter mass distributions.

Result: Nine long-lived clumps were identified after excluding the initial transient phase; eight migrated inward while losing 60-90% of their initial mass. The analytical model reproduces the overall dependence of migration on clump mass and galactocentric radius, but overestimates inspiral times by factors of ~2-10 for six clumps. Mass loss cannot explain the faster migration since it would reduce the dynamical friction force.

Conclusion: Additional physical processes—such as clump-clump interactions, non-circular orbits, and the time-dependent disk potential—play an important role in regulating clump migration and are not captured by the classical Chandrasekhar formulation, implying that this formalism is insufficient for accurately predicting clump inspiral in realistic disk galaxies.

Abstract: Massive stellar clumps are thought to migrate toward galactic centers through dynamical friction, contributing to bulge growth and the structural evolution of disk galaxies. While the classical Chandrasekhar formalism is widely used to estimate clump inspiral times, its validity for extended, evolving clumps embedded in realistic galactic disks remains uncertain. We test this formalism using an isolated disk galaxy simulation by identifying and tracking individual stellar clumps over multiple orbital periods. We develop a position- and mass-based tracking algorithm that follows long-lived clumps and compare their measured orbital evolution with predictions from a dynamical friction model constructed from the simulated baryonic and dark matter mass distributions. After excluding the initial transient phase, we identify nine long-lived clumps, eight of which migrate inward while losing 60-90\% of their initial mass. The analytical model reproduces the overall dependence of migration on clump mass and galactocentric radius, but for six clumps it overestimates the inspiral time by factors of $\sim2$-$10$. Since mass loss would reduce the dynamical friction force, it cannot account for the observed faster migration. Our results suggest that additional physical processes, for example clump-clump interactions, non-circular orbits, and the time-dependent disk potential play an important role in regulating clump migration beyond the assumptions of the classical Chandrasekhar formulation.

</details>
