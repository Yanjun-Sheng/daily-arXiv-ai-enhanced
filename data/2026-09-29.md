  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Large-scale magnetic reversals in planets, stars, and accretion disks lack a nonlinear theory coupling the magnetic field to its sustaining flow, motivating an exact, self-consistent treatment of the reversal mechanism.

Method: The authors derive exact nonlinear solutions of incompressible magnetohydrodynamics in a background rotational shear, assuming large-scale velocity and magnetic fields linear in position (affine form). These solutions expose a cyclic feedback among meridional circulation, shear, magnetic tension, induction, and the Coriolis force; the steady-circulation reversal orbit (dynamo wave) is then evaluated for solar near-surface shear layer and tachocline conditions.

Result: The reversal orbit periodically reverses all magnetic components and supports slower modulations. In the solar NSSL, moderate shear gives stable 11-year reversals with poleward speeds of 8-12 m/s and poloidal/toroidal amplitudes of 4-11 G and 2.2-3.8 kG near 1 Mm depth, consistent with regional observations. At stronger shear, an 88-year reversal shows a 203-207-year modulation, reproducing Gleissberg and Suess-de Vries timescales but requiring weaker poloidal fields. At tachocline density, an 11-year reversal and the inferred return flow require toroidal fields of hundreds of kilogauss, exceeding large-scale-field reconstructions though partly allowed by seismic upper limits.

Conclusion: The results favor a shallow NSSL origin for a local solar cycle while leaving the global dynamo open. Toroidal dominance prolongs the cycle because shear on a weak poloidal field changes only a small fraction of the stronger toroidal field per rotation; balancing weak magnetic torque against advection and the Coriolis force requires slow circulation that gradually turns the poloidal field and reverses toroidal induction. Efficient poloidal regeneration would shorten this delay, so a long solar cycle favors a primarily shear-driven dynamo.
